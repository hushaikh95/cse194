Why is rent so high? Competing explanations

Researchers and advocates disagree about what drives California's rent-versus-wage gap and what to do about it. Below is a short, balanced summary of the main explanations. We list them without ranking them, and each one has evidence for it and critics.

Not enough homes have been built

California's Legislative Analyst's Office (2015) concluded that the state built far fewer homes than demand called for in its coastal metros for decades. It pointed to local zoning, lengthy approval processes, environmental review, and fees. The state's housing department (HCD) has set a goal of 2.5 million new homes by 2030.

Debate: Some argue that new market-rate housing mostly serves higher earners and does little for low-wage renters in the short run. Others cite research that new supply lowers nearby rents over time.

Building is expensive

UC Berkeley's Terner Center reports that land, labor, materials, and financing costs in California are among the highest in the country. Higher interest rates since 2022 have made many projects harder to finance.

Debate: People disagree about how much of that cost comes from regulation, which policy can change, and how much from market forces.

Strong demand in high-wage regions

Job growth in tech, health care, and other high-paying industries brings in workers who can afford higher rents. This pushes up prices for everyone in the same housing market, including lower-wage workers.

Debate: Population growth has slowed and some counties lost residents after 2020, yet rents kept rising in many of them.

Wages at the bottom have not kept up

Some argue that the gap is as much about pay as about rent. Even after large minimum-wage increases since 2014, a full-time minimum-wage job covers well under half of a two-bedroom rental in most coastal counties.

Debate: Economists disagree about how high minimum wages can go before employers cut jobs or hours (see the Congressional Budget Office's 2019 review). Voters rejected an $18 statewide minimum wage (Prop 32) in 2024.

Rules for renters and landlords

Supporters say rent caps like AB 1482 and stronger eviction protections give tenants stability. Critics say strict rent control discourages new rental housing. A widely cited Stanford study of San Francisco (Diamond, McQuade & Qian, 2019) found that rent control helped covered tenants stay in their homes but also led some landlords to take units off the rental market.

Debate: Voters rejected expanding local rent control three times (2018, 2020, 2024), while many cities still have their own ordinances.

Housing as an investment

Some point to investors and large corporate landlords, short-term rentals, and low interest rates before 2022 as forces that pushed prices up. Others find these effects are small or limited to certain neighborhoods compared with the overall shortage.

Debate: Measuring this is hard, and research results vary by city.

Methods and sources

What the numbers mean

  • Two-bedroom rent is HUD's Fair Market Rent (FMR) for each county's rental area. It estimates a modest rental including utilities, at about the 40th percentile of local rents. FMRs use HUD's federal fiscal year (FY2026 starts October 2025).
  • Housing wage = monthly rent × 12 ÷ 0.30 ÷ 2,080 hours. This is the hourly pay a full-time worker needs so that rent takes no more than 30% of income, the same method NLIHC uses.
  • Hours per week at minimum wage = housing wage × 40 ÷ that year's state minimum wage (the rate for employers with 26+ workers from 2017 to 2022). Some cities set a higher local minimum wage, which this site doesn't include.
  • Job pay is the BLS median hourly wage for the county's metro or rural area. Counties in the same area share the same pay. Madera County uses the Fresno area for 2025 because BLS changed its area boundaries.
  • In 2025 dollars adjusts using the CPI-U annual average. The 2026 annual CPI isn't out yet, so 2026 is shown at 2025 prices. Hours of work don't change, because rent and wages are adjusted by the same amount.
  • Statewide figures are averages of the 58 counties, weighted by renter households. For 2026 the result is within a few dollars of NLIHC's published statewide figure.
  • Cost burden = share of renter households paying 30% or more of income on rent and utilities (50% or more = severe). Rental vacancy = vacant-for-rent units ÷ all rental units. Both are 5-year ACS estimates with margins of error, especially in small counties.

How figures were checked

Each figure is pulled from the official file by a script and checked against the source: row counts, column sums, and spot checks against published tables. For example, every 2026 county rent matches NLIHC's Out of Reach 2026. A second team member reviews each data file before it's published.

Framing

This site reports figures; it does not argue for any policy. Labels are kept neutral, and the explanations section gives competing views side by side.
